## What to do when a client leaves you a bad Google review (before you respond angry)

Saturday at 9 AM you received a one-star on Google. The client says you arrived late, that you didn't solve anything and that they "would never return". Your first impulse is to write a four-paragraph defense explaining that he wasn't there either when you arrived. Don't do it.

81% of consumers check Google reviews before visiting a business ([wiserreview.com](https://wiserreview.com/blog/online-review-statistics/)). They don't just read the bad review: 89% read your response ([bazaarvoice.com](https://www.bazaarvoice.com/blog/how-to-respond-to-negative-reviews-examples-and-best-practices/)). If you respond angry or don't respond, you lose both clients: the one who complained and the fifty who read after.

## What changed in 2026

- 73% of consumers only trust reviews written in the last month ([wiserreview.com](https://wiserreview.com/blog/google-review-statistics/)). A pile of one hundred old reviews doesn't compensate for three bad ones from last week.
- Businesses in the top 3 of Google local results average 47 reviews ([replyonthefly.com](https://www.replyonthefly.com/blog/how-many-google-reviews-do-you-need)). Fewer than five reviews and your star rating doesn't even appear.
- You have full control over how you respond, and your business profile must be verified so you can respond ([support.google.com](https://support.google.com/business/community-guide/318603570/how-to-effectively-respond-to-google-reviews-tips-and-best-practices?hl=en)).
- The client who left the review receives a notification when you respond, and can change their rating after reading your response ([support.google.com](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474050?hl=en-GB)).

## How this looks in practice

You respond from your Google Business Profile without paying anyone. You enter your account, navigate to the dashboard, tap "read reviews", scroll down to the review you need, tap "respond" and enter your text ([support.google.com](https://support.google.com/business/community-guide/318603570/how-to-effectively-respond-to-google-reviews-tips-and-best-practices?hl=en)).

The response you publish appears below the original review, visible to anyone who searches for your business on Google Maps or Search. Never share private client information in your public response; if the situation is complex, ask them to contact you by phone or email to resolve it privately ([support.google.com](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122?hl=en)).

The three pieces of a useful response:

1. **Acknowledge the specific problem the client mentions**. If they say they waited thirty minutes, don't say "we regret your experience". Say "we regret that you waited thirty minutes without an update from us".

2. **Explain what you do differently now**. Don't promise that "we'll work on improving". Negative reviews are an opportunity to understand client expectations and improve future experiences, and responding constructively can motivate the client to update their review ([support.google.com](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122?hl=en)). Mention the concrete action: "we changed the appointment confirmation process to avoid this problem".

3. **Move the conversation out of public view**. Give them your direct email or your WhatsApp Business line so they can keep talking. Some clients just want to be heard; when they see you respond fast and serious, the anger drops.

Keep your responses professional, clear and useful; also keep them short and simple, because if a response is too long people stop reading before they reach the main point ([support.google.com](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122?hl=en)).

## When NOT to use a long and detailed response

If the review is clearly false—someone who was never your client, who mentions a service you don't offer, or who copies and pastes the same text in ten different businesses—don't enter into public debate. Never get into a public argument, it's a battle you can't win; instead, report the review for removal using the platform's tools and while you wait for the decision publish a single calm and professional response ([adwave.com](https://adwave.com/resources/responding-to-negative-reviews-templates-and-best-practices)).

If the client asks for a refund or a discount in the public review, don't offer it there. Avoid offering specific compensation like refunds or discounts in your public response, because although it seems like the fastest way to resolve the problem it can create a dangerous precedent: you don't want to teach people that complaining publicly is the easiest way to get something free ([adwave.com](https://adwave.com/resources/responding-to-negative-reviews-templates-and-best-practices)). Your public response shows willingness to resolve, but the arrangement is negotiated privately.

If you receive three bad reviews in one week that mention the same operational problem—delay in responding to messages, parking problems, outdated hours—the error isn't in how you respond: it's in what you didn't fix. Respond to each review, but dedicate more time to correcting the root of the problem than to writing apologies.

## The stack we use at Impulsa Lab to automate responses (when volume justifies it)

If you have a single location and receive fewer than ten reviews per month, respond manually from the Google Business Profile dashboard. It's free, it works and you don't need code.

If you have multiple locations or receive more than twenty reviews weekly, automating the first draft of the response saves you between four and six hours per week.

We run a flow in **n8n** (self-hosted, 20 USD/month on Digital Ocean, or free if you already have a server) that:

1. Connects to the Google Business Profile API every two hours to pull new reviews without responses.
2. Passes the review text to **Claude Sonnet 4** via API (Anthropic, 15 USD per million input tokens, approximately 0.003 USD per processed review).
3. Claude generates a response draft in Spanish that follows three rules: acknowledges the client's specific point, proposes a concrete action if applicable, closes with an invitation to private contact.
4. The draft is saved in **Airtable** (free up to 1,000 records/base) for you to review before publishing. We don't publish automatically: the owner or manager approves each response.
5. Once approved, the flow sends the response back to Google via API.

Total monthly cost for a business with three locations that receives forty reviews per month: 20 USD (server) + 0.12 USD (Claude) = approximately 21 USD. Time saved: five hours of manual writing. We never eliminate human review: Claude writes, you decide if it sounds like your business.

If you don't want to configure this yourself, tools like **Buffer** (from 6 USD/month/channel) or **Localith** allow you to respond to Google reviews from a unified dashboard, useful if you manage several locations but prefer to write manually.

## The most common error: only responding to the bad ones

A common operational error is responding only to unsatisfied clients; although it operationally makes sense, since Google tends to favor reviews with which there is interaction, by responding only to negative ones you risk pushing that content to the top of your page; avoid it by responding to all your clients ([widewail.com](https://www.widewail.com/guide-review-response)).

Three conditions to decide if it's worth responding to each review (positive or negative):

1. **You receive at least three reviews per month**. If you receive one every two months, responding to all doesn't move the needle.
2. **The time you invest in responding doesn't exceed thirty minutes weekly**. If responding becomes a part-time job, you're writing too much per review or you need to automate the draft.
3. **You see that recent reviews (last thirty days) influence your business conversions**. Ask three new clients this week how they found you and what they checked before contacting you; if they mention Google, it matters.

Respond first within six hours if it's one star, within twenty-four hours if it's two or three stars. Then respond to the positive ones when you have time; a "thanks for visiting us" of three lines is enough and keeps the good content visible.
