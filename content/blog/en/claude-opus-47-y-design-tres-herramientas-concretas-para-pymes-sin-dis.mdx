## When NOT to use Claude Design

We've tested the tool across 14 verticals. Three scenarios where you should stick with your current workflow:

**1. If your visual brand IS your competitive advantage**  
Creative agency, luxury product, architecture firm. Opus 4.7 has a persistent default design taste: warm cream or off-white backgrounds, serif typography for display, italic accents, and terracotta or amber highlights. That sounds cosmetic, but it has real product implications. If your client expects minimalist black/white Helvetica, you need to specify alternative system in each prompt or the model reverts to default.

**2. If you need pixel-perfect layer control**  
Mobile app design with 40+ interconnected screens, 80-page brand manual, reusable UI kit. Claude Design has its own weekly usage limits if you have a paid plan (Pro, Max, Team, Enterprise) and the system consumes many tokens. After building a design system and a news website prototype based on it, along with some adjustments and an explanatory video, I had already used more than 50% of my weekly allocation. Past that limit, you enter pay-as-you-go costs per token.

**3. If your flow has more than three handoffs**  
Sketch → senior designer → brand lead → legal → executive committee. Each iteration in Design consumes tokens; feedback from six stakeholders in series can burn 80% of your weekly limit. In that case, collaborative Figma remains more economical.

## The most common mistake

"I tried Claude Design, it came out horrible, I went back to Canva."

The problem isn't the tool. It's that they treated Design like Canva with voice. Canva starts from templates and modifies. Design starts from system and generates.

Three fit conditions for it to work:

**A. You have minimum brand files**  
Logo in SVG or high-resolution PNG, palette (hex codes), two defined typefaces. If you don't have that, Design invents; what it invents may not match your implicit expectation.

**B. Your prompt describes constraints, not just desires**  
Bad: "Professional presentation of financial services."  
Good: "8-slide presentation for CFO of manufacturing SMB. Palette: navy blue #1A3A52, gray #6B7280. Font: Inter. Include bar chart of ROI on slide 3. Tone: hard data, zero fluff."

"You don't need to be a designer to get good results. Be specific about what you're building, who it's for, and what matters most," says Anthropic's documentation.

**C. You use iterative workflow, not one-shot**  
First pass in Design (10 min) → export to Canva → team adjustments (15 min) → re-import context if you need new slide. Don't expect first output to be final; the value is in the five iterations that previously took two days.

## Three concrete flows

**Flow 1: Pitch deck from scratch (B2B SaaS, 40 employees)**

1. Gather inputs: pitch script (Google Doc), logo, product screenshot, competitor's previous deck (PDF)  
2. Prompt in Claude Design:

"12-slide pitch deck for Series A. Audience: enterprise software VCs. Company: employee onboarding platform for hospitals. Problem: 40% of nurses quit in first year due to poor onboarding. Solution: automated workflows + AI check-ins. Traction: 18 clients, 140K ARR, 25% MoM. Palette: health green #00A896, white, dark gray. Typography: Manrope. Include unit economics slide and cohort retention. Style: clean, data-driven, slides with one central idea each."

Attach: logo.svg, screenshot-dashboard.png, competitor-deck.pdf (as visual reference)

3. Claude generates first version (2 min)  
4. Iterate: "Slide 4 too dense. Split into two slides. Slide 7: change line chart to stacked bars."  
5. Export to PPTX, open in PowerPoint or Google Slides for final team adjustments  

Time: 35 minutes. Before: 3 days (external designer) or 8 hours (internal struggling in Canva).

**Flow 2: Mobile app prototype (restaurant, 12 employees)**

Owner of Peruvian restaurant in Miami wants ordering app. Doesn't have budget for Figma + designer (4,000-8,000 USD concept phase).

Attach: restaurant-logo.png, 3 dish photos  

2. Claude generates navigable interactive prototype
